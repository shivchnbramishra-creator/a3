Linguistics basics: a friendly introduction

The core branches of linguistics explained in plain language, with simple examples you can test on your own speech.

Getting started

Thinking like a linguist

Linguists are descriptive: they observe how people actually speak and sign, then look for the patterns underneath. Instead of asking “Is this correct?”, a linguist asks “What rule is this speaker following?”

Every native speaker carries an enormous amount of unconscious knowledge. You know that “blick” could be an English word but “bnick” couldn’t, even though you’ve never been taught that rule. Linguistics makes this hidden knowledge visible.

Chapter 1

Phonetics and phonology

Phonetics studies the physical sounds of speech: how they are made with the lips, tongue and vocal folds, and how they travel and are heard. Phonology studies how each language organises sounds into a system.

Say pin and spin with your hand in front of your mouth. You’ll feel a puff of air after the “p” in pin but not in spin. English speakers hear them as the same sound, a single phoneme, but in some languages the difference changes meaning.

The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) gives linguists one symbol for each sound, so pronunciation can be written consistently across languages.

Chapters 2 & 3

Morphology and syntax

Morphology looks inside words. “Cats” contains two morphemes: cat and the plural -s. Some languages build very long words from many morphemes, while others use mostly single-morpheme words.

Syntax studies how words combine into phrases and sentences. Word order carries meaning in English:

The dog chased the cat and The cat chased the dog use the same words but describe different events. Languages such as Latin and Russian use word endings to show who did what, allowing freer word order.

Chapters 4 & 5

Semantics and pragmatics

Semantics studies literal meaning: what words refer to, how meanings relate (synonyms, opposites) and how sentence meaning is built from its parts. It also explains ambiguity:

I saw the man with the telescope. Who had the telescope? The words allow two structures, and so two meanings.

Pragmatics studies meaning in context. If someone says “It’s cold in here” near an open window, they may really be asking you to close it. Politeness, implication and turn-taking are all part of pragmatics.

Sociolinguistics

How language varies by region, social group, age and setting, and how it signals identity.

Psycholinguistics

How the mind produces and understands language, and how children acquire it so quickly.

Computational linguistics

How computers can model language, powering tools like spell-checkers and machine translation.

Chapter 6

Historical linguistics

All languages change. Sounds shift, words gain and lose meanings, and grammar evolves. By comparing related languages systematically, linguists can reconstruct parts of ancestor languages that were never written down.

English father, Latin pater and Sanskrit pitar correspond regularly, pointing to a shared Indo-European ancestor. The change from “p” to “f” in Germanic languages is part of a pattern known as Grimm’s Law.
